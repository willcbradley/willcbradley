Will Bradley

Non-Meta-Problems

Figuring out what to work on is hard.

In hindsight, many of the best opportunities of history seem random. You couldn't plan the discovery of penicillin, or predict the emergence of the internet. And yet working in one field or another, one company or another, one project or another, would've made all the difference.

Over time, this will only become more pronounced. Tech increases leverage, which increases the rewards of choosing the right option (and increases the cost of taking the wrong ones). The ground is shifting beneath our feet, and the path forward has never been narrower.

How to deal with this uncertainty? A common response is to gravitate towards meta-problems: the problem of what problem to work on.

In theory, solving these meta-problems lets us rule out unpromising opportunities, increasing our odds of doing great work asymptotically towards 100%. But in excess, this process can turn into an impossible, endless search for certainty. A way of keeping the option of everything, without ever becoming anything.

Is there a balance? And if so, how do we strike it?

The line between problems and meta-problems can be blurry.

Everything we do informs everything we do next, so there isn't always a clear boundary between "working" and "figuring out what to work on".

This blurriness puts us at risk of doing fake work. Pouring effort into things that may look useful, but are devoid of real-world feedback loops.

So, what if we did the opposite of that?

If fake work has no feedback loops, then real work is only feedback loops. It keeps us in contact with nature, or human nature, or both, and lets reality correct our errors.

To the extent that work has these feedback loops, it's real. And to the extent that it's real, we know we're dealing with non-meta-problems.

But the question remains: which non-meta-problems to work on?

Unrealised potential is corrosive.

Life is finite, and options are perishable. So we're forced to risk time and effort on projects, with no guarantee that it'll pay off.

The decisive skill, then, isn't picking correctly, but iteration speed. The pace at which we can work, generate new data from contact with reality, and adapt accordingly.

The key here is not to get stuck in the middle. It makes sense to be doing low-level work, or high-level strategy, but nothing in between.

Of course, it'd be nice if we could just pick the right direction from the outset. But things change too quickly for this to be possible — by the time we've identified the correct direction, it's already shifted. There's certainly some value in trying to make a decent guess. But the faster the world moves, the faster these returns diminish.

So "the right direction" isn't a direction at all, but rather a process.

I wrote this essay to figure out if it was worthwhile to write more essays.

To maximise iteration speed, it makes sense to only publish thoughts about big, important, thorny problems that I can't otherwise map out.

And with this meta-meta-problem out of the way, I can finally get down to business.
