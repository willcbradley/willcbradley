How To Decide What To Work On

Figuring out what to work on is hard.

In hindsight, many of history's best opportunities seem random. You couldn't plan the discovery of penicillin, or predict the growth of the internet. And yet working in one field or another, one company or another, one team or another, would've made all the difference.

Over time, this will only become more pronounced. Tech increases leverage, which makes the right choices better, and the wrong choices worse. The ground is shifting underneath us, and the path forward has never been narrower.

How to deal with this uncertainty? A common response is to focus on meta-problems: the problem of what problem to work on.

In theory, solving these meta-problems lets us avoid bad opportunities, increasing our odds of success asymptotically toward 100%. But in excess, this turns into an impossible search for certainty. A way of keeping every option open, without ever becoming anything.

Is there a balance? And if so, how do we strike it?

If we don't know where we're going, we can't get there.

We can think about what to do, or how to do it, but only one at a time. Doing them simultaneously is impossible, for the same reason it's impossible to travel the world by simply staring at a map, or draw a new map by simply travelling the world.

It sound obvious when you put it like that, but in practice it doesn't feel obvious. There's just enough overlap between thinking and doing for us to convince ourselves we're making headway. So if we aren't careful, we fall into a trap of fake work: expending effort in the absence of real-world feedback loops.

What if we did the opposite of that?

If fake work has no feedback loops, then real work is only feedback loops. It keeps us in contact with nature, or human nature, or both, and lets reality correct our errors.

To the extent that work has these feedback loops, it's real.

But this doesn't solve the other side of our equation. How do we figure out what to work on in the first place?

Life is finite, and options are perishable. Our backs are against the wall, so we're forced to spend time and effort, despite no guaranteed payoff.

This means the decisive skill isn't choosing perfectly, but iteration speed. How quickly we can alternate between problems and meta-problems, without getting stuck in the middle.

Of course, it'd be nice if we could just pick the right direction from the start. But things change too quickly — by the time we've identified the correct path, it's shifted. There's some value in making a decent first guess, but the faster the world moves, the faster these returns diminish.

So "the right direction" isn't a direction at all, but rather a process.

The only wrong move is to do nothing.
