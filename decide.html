What To Work On?

Figuring out what to work on is hard.

In hindsight, many of history's best opportunities seem random. You couldn't predict the discovery of penicillin, or plan the growth of the internet. And yet working in one field or another, one company or another, one team or another, would've made all the difference.

Over time, this will only become more pronounced. Tech increases leverage, which makes the right choices better, and the wrong choices worse. The ground is shifting underneath us, and the path forward is getting narrower.

How to deal with this uncertainty? One option is to focus on meta-problems: the problem of what problem to work on.

In theory, solving these meta-problems lets us avoid bad opportunities, increasing our odds of success closer and closer to 100%. But in excess, this turns into an impossible search for certainty. A way of keeping every option open, without ever doing anything.

Is there a balance? If so, how do we strike it?

If we don't know where we're going, we can't get there.

We can think about what to do, or how to do it, but only one of these at a time. We can't do them simultaneously, for the same reason we can't travel the world by simply drawing a map.

It sound obvious when you put it like that, but in practice it doesn't feel obvious. There's just enough overlap between thinking and doing for us to convince ourselves we're making headway. So if we aren't careful, we fall into a trap of fake work: stuck between planning and working, with no real-world feedback loops.

What if we did the opposite of that?

If fake work has no feedback loops, then real work is only feedback loops. It keeps us in contact with nature, or human nature, or both, and lets reality correct our errors.

To the extent that work has these feedback loops, it's real.

But this doesn't solve the other side of our equation. How do we figure out what to work on in the first place?

Opportunities don't last forever.

There's no risk-free option. Our lives are finite, so we're forced to gamble time and effort, even though it might not pay off. There are certainly better and worse choices, but none are perfect. And without being tested in the real world, even a perfect plan is useless.

This means the most important factor isn't our choice of path, but our iteration speed. How quickly and effectively we can adjust course based on new information, without falling into fake work.

Of course, it'd be nice if we could just pick the best direction from the start. But the world is always changing — by the time we've identified the optimal path, it's moved. There's some value in making a decent first guess, but the faster the world moves, the faster these returns diminish.

So "the right direction" isn't a direction at all, but rather a process.

The only wrong move is to do nothing.
